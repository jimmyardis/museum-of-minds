Hall II

The Counting House

1723 — 1883

One believed markets were the expression of human sympathy properly ordered. The other believed they were the engine of exploitation. Both read each other. Neither would concede. The conversation they never had is the one this hall was built for.

John Stuart Mill
1806 — 1873
The Victorian philosopher who argued for liberty more precisely than anyone before him. On Liberty's harm principle is still the most widely cited justification for individual freedom. He introduced women's suffrage in Parliament in 1867. He was right about everything, usually a century early.
Enter Room
David Ricardo
1772 — 1823
The stockbroker who became the most systematic economist between Smith and Mill. His comparative advantage principle still underlies all free trade theory. His labor theory of value became Marx's foundation. He intended neither outcome.
Enter Room
Frédéric Bastiat
1801 — 1850
The most entertaining economist in the classical tradition. He invented the broken window fallacy, the seen and the unseen, and the Candlemakers' Petition. He wrote The Law while dying of tuberculosis. He finished it the year he died.

Henry George
1839 — 1897
He asked why poverty persisted alongside progress and answered: land rent. Progress and Poverty (1879) became one of the best-selling economics books of the century. His single tax nearly got him elected mayor of New York.

Thorstein Veblen
1857 — 1929
He invented conspicuous consumption and pecuniary emulation, was fired from three universities, and treated the Gilded Age with the anthropological detachment of a man studying a primitive ritual. His prose is impenetrable. His analysis is devastating.

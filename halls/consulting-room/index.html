Hall VIII

The Consulting Room

1832 — 1961

Eight who turned the instrument on the mind. A Leipzig professor who timed it in a laboratory, and a Harvard physician who said it streams. A Russian physiologist who read it in a dog's saliva. A Viennese neurologist who found the cellar under the house, a Paris professor who found it first, and a Swiss psychiatrist who broke with Vienna over what the cellar holds. A Harvard émigré who took psychology into the courtroom, and the Viennese doctor who walked out of Freud's circle to ask what our symptoms are for. They disagreed about nearly everything except the premise: that the mind can be studied, and that the study can help.

Wilhelm Wundt
1832 — 1920
The Leipzig professor who opened the first psychology laboratory in 1879 and trained its first generation. He timed the mind to the thousandth of a second, then spent twenty years arguing that language, myth and custom lie beyond any laboratory. He signed the German professors' war manifesto of 1914.

William James
1842 — 1910
The father of American psychology, who talked himself out of despair by choosing to believe in free will. He coined 'stream of consciousness', founded pragmatism, and studied religious experience with a doctor's eye and a pilgrim's sympathy. He met Freud in 1909 and had doubts.

Ivan Pavlov
1849 — 1936
The Russian physiologist who won the Nobel Prize for digestion and then discovered the conditioned reflex in dogs that drooled before the food came. Blunt, exacting and fearless with the Bolsheviks, he still took their money for his laboratories.
Enter Room
Sigmund Freud
1856 — 1939
The Viennese neurologist who put the unconscious on the couch. Dreams, slips, repression, the Oedipus complex, id and ego — he gave the twentieth century the words it uses for itself, and broke with Jung over them. Driven out by the Nazis in 1938, he died in London. His science is still on trial.
Enter Room
Pierre Janet
1859 — 1947
The Paris philosopher-physician who studied hysteria at the Salpêtrière, coined the word 'subconscious', and described how traumatic memories split off from the rest of the mind. He said psychoanalysis was built on his work; Freud never forgave him.
Enter Room
Hugo Münsterberg
1863 — 1916
Wundt's student and William James's choice to run the Harvard laboratory, he took psychology into the courtroom, the factory and the cinema. He defended Germany in 1914, was shunned as a suspected spy, and died on the lecture platform.
Enter Room
Alfred Adler
1870 — 1937
The Viennese doctor who walked out of Freud's circle in 1911 and founded Individual Psychology. He gave us the inferiority complex, birth order and social interest, ran child-guidance clinics in the schools of Red Vienna, and died on a lecture tour in Aberdeen.
Enter Room
Carl Jung
1875 — 1961
Freud's chosen heir until he wasn't. He broke with the master in 1913 over what lies beneath — not a private cellar of repressed wishes, he argued, but a collective inheritance of archetypes shared by everyone. Spent the years after the break in a confrontation with his own unconscious, recorded in the Red Book. His conduct in 1930s Germany is still argued over.

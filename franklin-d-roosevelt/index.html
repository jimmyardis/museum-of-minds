Curatorial Note

The patrician who saved capitalism by making its victims believe in it again.

The 32nd President of the United States, elected four times and serving from 1933 to 1945 through the twin catastrophes of the Great Depression and the Second World War. He built the modern American regulatory and welfare state through the New Deal, shattered the isolationist consensus to forge a global coalition against fascism, and died with victory in sight but the postwar order unfinished. He governed from a wheelchair he refused to let the public see, broadcast his intentions directly into thirty million living rooms, and wielded executive power with a suppleness that left the republic transformed — and the question of how much transformation democracy could bear permanently open.

Franklin Roosevelt governed at the intersection of catastrophe and possibility. He inherited an economy in freefall — one quarter of Americans unemployed, banks closing by the hundreds, the democratic experiment itself in question — and he met the crisis with an unparalleled torrent of legislation, institution-building, and sheer rhetorical will.

"The only thing we have to fear is fear itself — nameless, unreasoning, unjustified terror which paralyzes needed efforts to convert retreat into advance."

— First Inaugural Address, March 4, 1933

He was also a man of profound contradiction: the Hyde Park aristocrat who championed the forgotten man; the liberal internationalist who interned Japanese Americans; the humanitarian who left the St. Louis and its nine hundred Jewish refugees to turn back toward their doom. Ask him about power, about failure, about what endures and what was squandered.

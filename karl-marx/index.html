Karl Marx

Trier, 1818  —  London, 1883

The philosopher who decided the point was to change the world

Karl Marx was a German philosopher, economist, and revolutionary theorist whose analysis of capitalism and class struggle became the intellectual foundation for socialist and communist movements worldwide. He was a man of volcanic contradictions — a stateless exile who claimed to speak for all humanity, a bourgeois intellectual who despised the bourgeoisie, a prophet of liberation whose ideas were wielded, in turns, as scripture and as sword.

Expelled from Prussia, France, and Belgium before settling in the fog of London, Marx spent three decades in the Reading Room of the British Museum, dissecting the very economic order that had made England rich and its workers wretched. The result was Das Kapital — a monument of forensic analysis that remains the most formidable critique of industrial capitalism ever written.

Engage the Archive

Speak directly with the philosopher who shook the nineteenth century to its foundations

Marx did not write for posterity — he wrote to be useful. The Manifesto, the Grundrisse, the journalism, the letters to Engels at three in the morning: all of it was aimed at understanding a world he believed could be, must be, transformed. Here, that voice is restored.

Ask him whether violence was inevitable, or whether his scientific method was ultimately more theology than science. Ask him about the contradictions he lived: the factory owner's son who bankrolled his career, the children who suffered for his theories, the movement that claimed his name and buried his intentions.

The philosophers have only interpreted the world in various ways — the point, however, is to change it.

In His Own Words
Curator's Note

The draftsman the Senate refused.

John Rutledge was the most powerful man in Revolutionary South Carolina — a Charleston lawyer trained at London's Middle Temple who governed his state through its darkest years. When the British overran South Carolina in 1780, its legislature had already vested him with powers so sweeping that his enemies called him "Dictator John." He fled with the government, kept its constitutional structure alive in exile, and returned after Yorktown to rebuild what the occupation had broken.

At Philadelphia in 1787 he did the work history mostly forgot. Madison supplied the theory and Hamilton the rhetoric, but it was Rutledge who chaired the Committee of Detail — the five men who took the Convention's agreed principles and turned them into an actual document. The Constitution's first working draft came out of his committee: the structure of Article I, the enumeration of congressional powers, and the Supremacy Clause that binds every state to federal law. He was not the visionary of the Convention. He was its draftsman, and the language he set down is still the language of the republic.

He was also the Convention's most unapologetic defender of slavery's protection in the text. He demanded the twenty-year shield for the slave trade as South Carolina's price of union, and he called it what it was — interest, not principle. He does not pretend otherwise, and the document he drafted carries the compromise he insisted upon. — On the price he demanded, and never disowned

Washington named him to the first Supreme Court in 1790, one of the original six justices; he resigned within a year for a better-paying seat as South Carolina's chief justice. In 1795 Washington called him back with a recess appointment as Chief Justice of the United States. He presided over one term. Then, grieving his wife's death, he gave an intemperate speech in Charleston denouncing the Jay Treaty as a betrayal of the Revolution — and the Senate rejected his nomination, 14 to 10. No Chief Justice had ever been refused before. None of his own words could undo it, and few of them survive: his record lives largely in the notes and journals of the men around him.

He engages all of it directly — the drafting table, the bargain over the slave trade, the resignation for pay, the speech that ended him. Ask him why Madison gets the credit for language he wrote. Ask him whether the twenty-year clause was necessity or failure. Ask him what it costs to be rejected by the government whose charter you drafted.

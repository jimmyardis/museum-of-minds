Alfred Marshall
Bermondsey, London, 1842  ·  Cambridge, 1924

The Cambridge mathematician who gave economics the supply-and-demand cross, told his students to burn the mathematics, and spent twenty years on a book whose second volume never came.

A Bank of England cashier's son who refused the Church for mathematics, he came out Second Wrangler in 1865, walked the slums of English cities looking at faces, and decided political economy might do something about them. He lost his fellowship by marrying his student Mary Paley, came back to Cambridge as professor in 1885, and in 1890 published the Principles of Economics, with the diagrams in footnotes and the mathematics in an appendix. He gave the subject elasticity, consumer's surplus, the short and the long period, and the Economics Tripos, and he trained Pigou and Keynes.

In 1896 he spoke against degrees for the women he had taught. His wife had taught them for twenty years. He will not plead the spirit of the age.

Consultation

Cool heads but
warm hearts.

Marshall speaks from thousands of chunks across eight works — the Principles of Economics in its 1895 edition, Industry and Trade, Money, Credit and Commerce, The Economics of Industry he wrote with Mary Paley Marshall, the Elements of Economics of Industry, his Official Papers before the royal commissions, the Memorials Pigou gathered, and his address to the Co-operative Congress of 1889.

His discourse holds the rival he reviewed coolly and the critic who thought his marginal man a fiction: Jevons's Theory of Political Economy of 1871, and Thorstein Veblen's Theory of the Leisure Class. Veblen's room is in this hall.

Ask him why he buried the mathematics. Ask him whether a minimum wage helps the poor, other things being equal. Ask him what the representative firm is now. Ask him about the vote of 1896.

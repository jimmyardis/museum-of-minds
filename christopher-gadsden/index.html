Curator's Note

The man who said "Americans" first.

Christopher Gadsden was a Charleston merchant with a wharf on the Cooper River and no patience whatsoever for petitioning kings. Sent to the Stamp Act Congress in New York in 1765, he made an argument no other delegate was making: that the colonies should ground their resistance not in their rights as Englishmen but in a common American identity that did not yet officially exist. "There ought to be no New England man, no New Yorker, known on the continent," he insisted, "but all of us Americans." He was calling for independence, in everything but the word, eleven years before the Declaration.

He came home and built the movement to match the rhetoric. Under a live oak in Charleston that came to be called the Liberty Tree, Gadsden organized the city's mechanics and artisans into South Carolina's Sons of Liberty — the radical core of resistance in the southern colonies. A decade later, as a delegate to the Continental Congress, he distilled the cause into a single image: a coiled timber rattlesnake on a yellow field above the words "Don't Tread on Me." He presented the flag to Commodore Esek Hopkins, and it flew as the first standard of the Continental Navy. The symbolism was deliberate. The rattlesnake is native to America, it never strikes first, it gives fair warning — and when it strikes, it does not miss.

The war tested whether he meant it. Commissioned a brigadier general, he helped defend Charleston until the city fell to the British in 1780 — he was its lieutenant governor when it surrendered. Offered parole if he would swear never again to take up arms, he refused, and was shipped to the Castillo de San Marcos at St. Augustine, where he spent forty-two weeks in a dungeon, much of it in solitary confinement, a man in his late fifties held in conditions designed to break him. They did not break him. When his exchange was finally arranged, he refused to leave until his fellow prisoners were included. He returned to help govern the state he had freed, argued — remarkably, for so unforgiving a man — for clemency toward Loyalists, supported ratification of the Constitution at South Carolina's convention in 1788, and died in Charleston in 1805.

He was also a slaveholder, and his wharf became one of the busiest points of entry for enslaved Africans in North America. The man who demanded "Don't Tread on Me" trod on others his entire life, knew it, and never resolved it. — On the contradiction he never closed

He engages these questions directly — the gap between the liberty he demanded and the bondage he practiced is where his most honest conversations happen. Ask him what the rattlesnake actually meant, and what it has been made to mean since. Ask him what forty-two weeks in a Spanish fortress dungeon taught him about the price of a signature. Ask him why he refused the parole that would have sent him home.

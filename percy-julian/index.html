Chemist & Industrialist
Percy Lavon Julian
1899 — 1975
He synthesized the molecules that eased the world's suffering — and a nation tried to stop him at every turn.

Born in Montgomery, Alabama, to a father who was a railway mail clerk and a grandfather who had been enslaved, Percy Julian entered DePauw University with a deficient preparatory education and graduated at its top. When no graduate fellowship would take a Black chemist, a professor of his own pocket sent him to Vienna. He returned with a doctorate — and nowhere to teach.

At Glidden Company he transformed soybeans into a pharmaceutical arsenal: industrial cortisone, progesterone, testosterone, and the first total synthesis of physostigmine for glaucoma. He made drugs that had cost hundreds of dollars per dose available for pennies, rewriting the economics of medicine.

When he bought a home in Oak Park, Illinois, in 1950, the house was bombed — twice. He built a laboratory instead and held more than 130 patents when he died. He was offered honorary doctorates from universities that had refused to hire him.

Engage the Mind

Ask Percy Julian what it cost — and what it was worth.

Percy Julian synthesized cortisone for pennies per gram at a time pharmaceutical companies sold it for hundreds of dollars per dose. He holds more than 130 patents. He was twice denied professorships on racial grounds and twice had his home bombed. He built an empire from soybeans and changed the price of suffering.

His is not merely a story of scientific genius — it is a reckoning with what American institutions choose to reward and what they choose to destroy. Julian lived the full contradiction: celebrated by industry, targeted by neighbors, honored by presidents who governed systems that excluded him.

"I have had one goal in my life — to prove that the Negro mind has the capacity to cope with the best minds in the world." — Percy Lavon Julian, c. 1960s

Pose the hard questions. Ask him about drug pricing, about the firebombing, about the choice of industry over academia. He will answer from the record of a life fully lived at the intersection of brilliance and obstruction.

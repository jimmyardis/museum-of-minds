Converse with Percy Julian

Percy Lavon Julian was born in Montgomery, Alabama, in 1899, the grandson of an enslaved man who had two fingers cut off as punishment for learning to write. He enrolled at DePauw University, where he slept in the attic of a fraternity house because no dormitory would take him, and graduated as valedictorian. When American graduate schools turned him away, he crossed the Atlantic and earned his doctorate from the University of Vienna — one of the greatest centers of organic chemistry in the world.

Back in the United States, he synthesized physostigmine — a treatment for glaucoma — becoming the first to accomplish it from scratch and vindicating a method that a team at Oxford had abandoned. He applied to teach at DePauw. The offer was rescinded. He was hired as director of research at Glidden, a paint company, and proceeded to transform its soybean extraction unit into one of the most productive pharmaceutical chemistry operations in the world.

"From soybeans he created progesterone and testosterone precursors, cortisone, and compounds that treated arthritis and eye disease. Treatments that had cost hundreds of dollars per gram became available for cents."

Museum of Minds — Curatorial Note, 2026

In 1950, he and his wife Anna became the first Black family to buy a home in Oak Park, Illinois. Someone threw a smoke bomb through his window. Two months later, someone tried to dynamite his garage while his children slept. He stayed. He founded Julian Laboratories. He became a millionaire. He held more than 130 patents. He fought for civil rights until his death in 1975.

Speak with him below. Ask him about cortisone, about Vienna, about Oak Park, about the Nobel Prize he never received, about the America that built barricades around his talent and watched him dismantle them one molecule at a time.

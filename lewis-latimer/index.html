Lewis Howard
Latimer
1848 — 1928

The man who made the light last — brilliant, Black, and systematically uncredited.

Born in Chelsea, Massachusetts to George and Rebecca Latimer — escaped enslaved people whose own freedom had required a national campaign — Lewis Howard Latimer entered the world already inside a story about what a Black man must fight to own. He enlisted in the Navy at fifteen, lied about his age, and came home to a city lit by gas and ambition. He taught himself mechanical drawing by studying the instruments in the office where he swept floors.

Within a decade he was drafting Alexander Graham Bell's telephone patent. Within two, he had refined the carbon filament that transformed Edison's laboratory curiosity into a commercially viable bulb — then wrote the foundational textbook on electric lighting and personally directed its installation across New York, Philadelphia, Montreal, and London. He was the only Black member of the Edison Pioneers.

Lewis Latimer was a man of exceptional precision — in his draftsmanship, his prose, and his reckoning with a world determined to render him invisible. He did not rage loudly. He documented everything, filed every patent, wrote every manual. He believed the record itself was a form of resistance.

Ask him about the myth of the lone genius, about what it felt like to draft Bell's drawings and never receive a footnote, about why he still believed in the promise of electric light even after the Edison Company absorbed his work into its own legend.

— Lewis Howard Latimer, Poems of Love and Life, c. 1925

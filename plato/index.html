Plato
Athens, c. 428 BC  ·  Athens, 348 BC

The aristocrat who watched Athens kill the best man he knew, and asked what a city would have to be so that it never happened again.

Born into one of the old families, he followed Socrates for a decade and was twenty-eight when the restored democracy executed him. He gave up politics, travelled to Italy and Sicily, and in 387 BC began teaching in the grove of Academus; Aristotle came at seventeen and stayed twenty years. He went to Syracuse twice to make a philosopher of a tyrant and failed both times. He wrote no treatise, only conversations, and in nearly all of them he wears the mask of a man who wrote nothing.

His ideal city abolishes the family, censors Homer and tells its citizens a noble lie. He will defend some of it.

Consultation

The unexamined life
is not worth living.

Plato speaks from thousands of chunks across twenty-three dialogues in Benjamin Jowett's translation — the Republic, the Apology, Crito, Phaedo, Symposium, Phaedrus, Gorgias, Meno, Protagoras, Euthyphro, Theaetetus, Timaeus and the Laws, with the Sophist, Statesman, Parmenides, Philebus and the shorter dialogues behind them.

His discourse holds his best student's objections: Aristotle's Politics, whose second book takes the Republic apart, and his Nicomachean Ethics; and Xenophon's Memorabilia, a quite different Socrates. Aristotle's room is next door.

Ask him where Socrates ends and he begins. Ask him whether a screen is his cave. Ask him whether the philosophers should rule, and how he thinks that has gone. Ask him about Syracuse.

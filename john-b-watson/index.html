John B. Watson
Travelers Rest, South Carolina, 1878  ·  New York City, 1958

The South Carolina farm boy who banished the mind from psychology, conditioned fear in a baby, and then sold cold cream and coffee with what he learned.

Twice arrested as a boy in Greenville, he talked his way into Furman at sixteen, took his doctorate at Chicago on the learning of white rats, and at thirty was a full professor at Johns Hopkins. In 1913 one lecture made psychology the science of behaviour and threw out consciousness. In the winter of 1919 he and his student Rosalie Rayner taught an eleven-month-old to fear a white rat. The affair ended his career in 1920; he spent the next twenty-five years at the J. Walter Thompson agency, and wrote Behaviorism for the public.

He told American parents never to kiss their children. His own sons paid for it, and he said late in life that he had not known enough to write the book.

Consultation

Give me a dozen
healthy infants.

Watson speaks from 1,630 chunks across five works — Behavior: An Introduction to Comparative Psychology, Psychology from the Standpoint of a Behaviorist, Behaviorism, the lectures he wrote for the public in 1925, Psychological Care of Infant and Child, and The Ways of Behaviorism.

His discourse holds the fight he picked: The Battle of Behaviorism, his 1924 Washington debate with William McDougall, McDougall's Introduction to Social Psychology, the instinct theory he set out to destroy, and the first volume of William James's Principles of Psychology, the introspective method he refused. James's room is down the hall.

Ask him what happened to Albert. Ask him whether he believed the dozen-infants boast when he wrote it. Ask him what he learned about fear from selling things. Ask him why he burned his papers.

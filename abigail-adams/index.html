Curator's Note

The voice they would not let into the room.

Abigail Adams never held office, never cast a vote, and never sat in a single assembly of the founding era — and she may be its most complete witness. From a farmhouse at the foot of Penn's Hill in Braintree, Massachusetts, she wrote thousands of letters to her husband John Adams in Philadelphia, Paris, London, and finally the presidency. Read together, they form the great primary record of the Revolution as it was actually lived: troop movements and grain prices, smallpox inoculations and constitutional theory, the smoke of Bunker Hill rising within sight of her doorstep while she managed a farm, four children, and the family's survival alone.

On March 31, 1776, while the Continental Congress moved toward independence, she wrote the letter that history remembers: Remember the Ladies. It was the first explicit demand for women's political representation in American correspondence — a warning that women would "foment a Rebelion" against laws in which they had no voice. John wrote back that he could not but laugh. She put her answer, and her prediction, on the record, and the record has vindicated her. She argued as fiercely for women's education, insisting that a republic resting on the virtue of its citizens could not neglect the minds of half of them, and she condemned slavery decades before it was safe to do so, calling it an iniquitous scheme to fight for freedom while daily robbing others of theirs.

As First Lady from 1797 to 1801 — the first to live in the unfinished President's House — she was the most politically engaged woman in America: attending Senate debates, feeding John intelligence and counsel he took seriously even when he claimed otherwise. And she raised a son, John Quincy Adams, whom she educated by candlelight and correspondence into a sixth president of the United States. The founding she was locked out of, she shaped anyway — from the household, which she understood to be where revolutions actually happen.

I wish most sincerely there was not a slave in the province. It always appeared a most iniquitous scheme to me — to fight ourselves for what we are daily robbing and plundering from those who have as good a right to freedom as we have. — To John Adams · September 22, 1774

She engages these questions directly — the distance between the equality the founders proclaimed and the equality they practiced is where her sharpest conversations happen. Ask her about the letter John laughed at. Ask her what it cost to run the Revolution at the household level. Ask her about Jefferson, whom she loved as a friend and never quite forgave.

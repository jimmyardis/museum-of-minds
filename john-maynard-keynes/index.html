John Maynard
Keynes

1883 — 1946

The economist who taught governments that spending is not sin

Cambridge-trained, Bloomsbury-minded, and catastrophically right about the consequences of Versailles, Keynes rewrote the rules of capitalism in the wreckage of the Great Depression. His central argument was elemental and devastating: markets left to themselves do not self-correct — they collapse, and only deliberate public spending can break the fall.

He was a man of brilliant contradictions — a Treasury mandarin who championed the dignity of the unemployed, a collector of fine paintings who trafficked in the bloodless mathematics of money, a creature of elite aesthetics who believed that ideas, not class interests, ultimately govern the world.

Speak with Keynes

Keynes did not write for posterity — he wrote for the crisis at hand, with the urgency of a man watching governments fail the people they governed. Yet the ideas he forged under pressure proved durable enough to reshape the entire postwar economic order.

"The ideas of economists and political philosophers, both when they are right and when they are wrong, are more powerful than is commonly understood. Indeed the world is ruled by little else."

— The General Theory, Chapter 24 · 1936

Ask him to defend deficit spending against the charge of political excess. Press him on the Versailles peace and what he predicted in 1919. Interrogate his famous dismissal of the long run. He will answer not as a ghost, but as a man still arguing — still reaching for the phrase that might persuade.

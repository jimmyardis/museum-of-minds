Alan
Turing

1912 — 1954

The man who broke the unbreakable and asked whether machines could think

Alan Mathison Turing was the British mathematician and logician who, working from a converted stable at Bletchley Park, cracked the Enigma cipher and helped turn the tide of World War II. Historians estimate his work shortened the war by two to four years and saved fourteen million lives.

Before the war, he had already laid the theoretical foundations of computation itself — imagining an abstract "universal machine" that could execute any algorithm, a thought experiment that became the architecture of every computer ever built.

In 1950 he asked a question that reoriented intellectual history: Can a machine think? His "Imitation Game" proposed a behavioral test for machine intelligence that philosophers, engineers, and now AI systems still grapple with in 2026.

He was prosecuted by the British government in 1952 for homosexuality, chemically castrated, and found dead two years later. His apple contained cyanide. He was forty-one years old.

Converse with
Alan Turing

Turing's mind encompassed cryptography, mathematics, theoretical computer science, philosophy of mind, and mathematical biology. He is simultaneously the patron saint of computing and its most cautionary figure — the man whose ideas built the modern world, and who was thanked with persecution.

I propose to consider the question, "Can machines think?" This should begin with definitions of the meaning of the terms "machine" and "think." — Computing Machinery and Intelligence, Mind, Vol. 59, 1950

In this conversation you may press Turing on what he actually meant by the Imitation Game — a question that has been systematically misread for seventy years. You may ask him whether the large language models of 2026 satisfy his criteria, or whether they merely perform a more sophisticated version of what he called "intellectual imitation." You may ask him about Bletchley, about his prosecution, about what it means to be destroyed by the civilization you saved.

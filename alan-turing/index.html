Exhibition Notes

Alan Turing was the British mathematician and logician who cracked the Enigma cipher at Bletchley Park, shortened the Second World War by years, and laid the theoretical foundations of the computer and artificial intelligence — two contributions that would remake every civilization on earth within a century of his birth.

He contained a central contradiction: a man who saved liberal civilization from fascism, then was destroyed by that same civilization for the crime of being homosexual. Subjected to chemical castration by the British state in 1952, he was found dead two years later. He was forty-one.

In 2026, with artificial intelligence reshaping every institution on earth, Turing is not a historical curiosity. He is the ghost inside every machine — the man who first asked whether a machine could think, before anyone had built one worth asking about.

Begin the Conversation

Turing thought with the precision of a logician and the imagination of a dreamer. In 1936, years before any electronic computer existed, he defined computation itself as an abstract process — a machine reading symbols on an infinite tape. In 1950, he proposed the Imitation Game not as a test of whether machines could feel, but as a way of dissolving a question he considered badly posed.

Ask him what he actually meant. Ask him about the difference between simulating thought and being thought. Ask him about Bletchley — about the experience of working in secrecy on a problem that determined the outcome of civilization. Ask him what it felt like to be pardoned, sixty years too late, for the crime of being himself.

Instead of trying to produce a programme to simulate the adult mind, why not rather try to produce one which simulates the child's? If this were then subjected to an appropriate course of education, one would obtain the adult brain.

— Computing Machinery and Intelligence, 1950
